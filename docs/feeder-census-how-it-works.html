Feeder Census · Junction 2026 · Arduino UNO Q

How Feeder Census works

A camera watches a winter bird feeder. A small model on an Arduino UNO Q names the birds in each photo and counts every species by the rule Finnish feeder monitoring already uses: the largest number seen at the same time. The counts stay on the device as open data, and a latch closes the feeder when a squirrel shows up.

The model described here ships since about 06:15 on 15 November 2026. Accuracy numbers and the student board timings on this page were measured on 15 November 2026, the off-the-shelf detector's timing on 14 November 2026. The demo plays recorded footage of one feeder camera or a live USB webcam; no frame of either is shown here.

What it consists of

Input

Camera

A USB camera aimed at the feeder. In the demo, recorded footage of a public feeder stream (1920 × 1080) stands in for it. At the hackathon stand a USB webcam looks at a wooden model feeder in a room; the model only knows the one feeder camera it was trained on.

UNO Q, Linux side

Detector model

One small neural network (YOLO26 nano, 960 × 960 px input, 2,540,204 parameters, about 10 MB, ONNX). The page calls it "BC 960". It finds each bird and names it in a single pass: 13 species plus "unidentified". Runs on the board's processor, no graphics card, no internet.

UNO Q, Linux side

Counting rule

Only birds named with at least 0.25 confidence (the cut-off), seen in two photos in a row, are counted. The count for a species is the largest number in one photo, per hour, day and half-month.

UNO Q, Linux side

Store and page

A small database on the board, and a local web page: the latest photo with boxes, counts, visits, hours watched, one evidence photo per species per day, CSV export.

UNO Q, microcontroller side

Latch

One servo on pin D9. The Linux side tells the microcontroller to close when a red squirrel is seen in photos in a row: by default two, with at least 0.5 confidence; at the stand three, with at least 0.8. If the commands stop, the latch reopens by itself after 30 s.

Off the device, once

Training pipeline

Large "teacher" models on rented GPUs labelled hours of feeder footage; the small "student" model learned from those labels. Only the student goes on the board.

What happens every 5 seconds

  1. Take one photo.One frame from the camera (in the demo, from the recording).
  2. Shrink it to 960 pixels and run the detector.About 1.1 s on the board in the benchmark; about 2.0 s per photo at the stand, where the board also runs the camera and a full-screen browser. Out come boxes, each with a species name and a confidence.
  3. Keep the sure ones.The detector itself drops every box under 0.25 (its floor). The cut-off is that same 0.25, so every box it reports can be counted.
  4. Confirm and count.A bird counts when it is there in two photos in a row. The largest number seen at once becomes the count for that hour, day and half-month.
  5. Save and show.Counts, visits and an evidence photo go to the database; the page shows the photo with its boxes until the next one replaces it.
  6. Check for a squirrel.Photos in a row with a red squirrel close the latch (two by default, three at the stand, where the squirrel must also have at least 0.8 confidence); five clear photos open it again.

How the model was made

  1. Collect footage.38 days of one feeder camera's stream (October and November 2026, plus two days from earlier winters), two videos of forest feeders, and squirrel material: 400 Creative Commons photos, frames of one documentary, and cut-outs pasted into feeder frames.
  2. Pick the frames with birds.Long streams are mostly an empty feeder. A miner keeps the moments where something moves. The 38-day training set has 103,798 frames.
  3. Let big models label them.A large detector and two detectors tuned to this camera draw the boxes. Two bird classifiers (BioCLIP 2 and an iNaturalist classifier) give the names.
  4. Check the labels with a second AI model.Boxes from the old labels, three earlier students and a camera-tuned teacher are pooled into one candidate per bird. An open vision-language model (Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct) looks at a crop of each candidate. Where all five sources already agreed, it named the same species in 96.5 % of cases, too weak to judge alone, so a bird is kept only when two votes agree on its species. Unclear cases become ignore zones, blurred out of training frames. "No animal" is dropped, except when the old label, the teacher and at least two earlier students all saw a bird there; then it becomes an ignore zone. No person checked the result. The 13 days added last were checked the same way (53,803 candidates judged, 42,105 checked frames added). The training boxes per species in the model's card (models/student/feeder.json) add up to 102,142; great tit 45,223 of them, willow tit 39.
  5. Train the small model on those labels.The 960 px student that had been trained on the 25 checked days trained 8 more epochs on all 38 days. The checked labels have no "unidentified" examples. One training frame in four also has a blurred, noisy copy so a worse camera does not throw it off.
  6. Measure on a day it never saw, time it on the board, choose by a fixed rule.The cut-off (0.25, the lowest value of the grid) is picked on 7,599 frames from 13 days of October 2026, never on the test day. The model later trained on other frames of those same days, so the cut-off is picked on days it has seen. A model ships only if it runs in under 1.5 s on the board.

Species the model can name: great tit, blue tit, bullfinch, jay, magpie, tree sparrow, nuthatch, house sparrow, great spotted woodpecker, crested tit, coal tit, willow tit, and red squirrel.

Squirrel and latch

  • The test day has no squirrel. Even so, the shipped model drew at least one squirrel box on it.
  • The real squirrel in the held-out clip of 22 October 2026 scores 0.86 on average (0.34 to 0.96) when it is in clear view. That clip was kept out of training, but the model has seen the same squirrel on other visits: in the first 25 training days, after the label check, 936 boxes, nearly all from 8, 22 (its second visit that day) and 31 October and 6 and 11 November 2026.
  • At the stand the webcam looks at a wooden model feeder in a room. There the model drew "Red squirrel" boxes on a person and on the wooden box at 0.55 to 0.60 confidence, and the hatch went up and down. Since then the board needs three photos in a row at 0.8 or more. After that change the hatch closed once in the first five minutes and not at all in the next four; what set off that one closing is not known. This is not solved.
  • The model has no rat class, so the latch reacts to squirrels only.

What we tried, and what helped

Five more days of the same camera that no model trained on (3, 9, 14, 19 and 25 October 2026, 2,808 frames, same automatic labels) served as exam days. They were scored with an ad-hoc scorer that picks its cut-off on the scored frames themselves (0.30 to 0.90), so the numbers run a little high and are only good for comparing models with each other. They were used to compare models and to choose what to train next, so they are not an untouched test either.

Frames counted exactly right. On 12 November the ad-hoc scorer gives lower numbers than the official evaluation (0.887 against 0.909 for the shipped model); its grid starts at 0.30, while the official cut-off is 0.25. The small 640 px student scored 0.887 officially, at cut-off 0.30.

What helped: reading 960 px instead of 640 px, and 13 more checked days of the same camera (about +3 points on 12 November, seen again in a second training run; under one point on the exam days). What did not help: an automatic search over training settings (16 variants per generation, 18 generations; 0.926 on the days it was tuned on, 0.906 on the exam days, below the plain model); a bigger model (same accuracy, 1,479 ms on the board against 1,111 ms); longer training; and 2,948 checked frames from other feeders' Creative Commons streams (Finland, Russia, the UK, Germany, Poland). An earlier test with 19 more days of uncleaned labels changed nothing; with checked labels, more days did help. Only two 2026 stream days of this camera remain unused.

What these numbers do not say

  • The test day is the same feeder and camera as the training footage. Nothing here says how the model does at another feeder, with another camera, or in another season.
  • The truth labels are machine-made and checked by a second AI model. No person checked them; the AI assistant that ran the work looked at sample sheets.
  • The test labels and the training labels come from the same automatic check, so the test rewards agreeing with that check. An earlier student's number on the same labels was measured another way (ad-hoc scorer, cut-off 0.50), so it is not a direct comparison.
  • 392 of the 2,256 candidates on the test day (17 %) are ignore zones: cut-off birds, blurred flight, sources that disagree. They are not scored, so the test is easier than counting every bird.
  • We do not claim it names species better than a skilled volunteer.
  • Only 12 November 2026 and the five exam days are days the shipped model never trained on. Its cut-off was picked on frames of days it did train on (other frames of them).
  • The demo reel is recorded, not live. It belongs to the stream's uploader and is not in our repository. With the live webcam at the stand the model sees a wooden model feeder in a room, which is not the camera it was trained on.

Next steps, not built

  • Give the bird more pixels: a camera closer to the feeder, or cut out the feeder region before the detector sees the photo.
  • Accept a less sure name when several photos in a row agree.
  • A small hand-checked test set: every accuracy number here is measured against machine-checked labels.
  • A microphone as a second witness, and as the trigger that wakes the camera.
