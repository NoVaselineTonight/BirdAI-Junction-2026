# Summary

Across the eleven sources, birds are used as indicators of six things: contaminant loads in food webs, the state of marine prey stocks, freshwater quality, habitat condition and human disturbance, wider biodiversity trends in farmland and forest, and whether ecosystems still have the species that carry out functions such as seed dispersal and scavenging.

Yes, these indications can be linked to environmental change, but with different levels of confidence:

- **Best supported:** farmland bird declines and agricultural intensification. UK farmland birds fell by about 40% from the mid-1970s, and the authors attribute this mainly to intensification [10].
- **Supported for specific cases:** forest bird communities and human disturbance of forest [4]; birds as integrators of pollution over space, time and food webs [6]; seabirds and marine productivity [1, ch. 6].
- **Main caveat:** bird monitoring is mostly correlational, not experimental [2]. Naming the cause of a change needs extra knowledge of population processes [1, ch. 7], and bird indices capture only part of biodiversity change [11].

# Why birds are used as indicators

**Strengths**

- Birds are widely monitored, easy to survey, and respond in predictable ways to environmental change [9].
- They respond to human-caused change, are well studied, have long data series, and matter to the public [11].
- Birds sit high in food chains, so they can integrate conditions over large areas, long periods and whole food webs. This can reduce the sampling effort needed [6].
- Their high food-chain position may make them a better early warning of hazards to humans than most invertebrates [6].
- Seabirds are conspicuous, wide-ranging top consumers, and are much easier to count than fish or marine mammals [1, ch. 6].
- Composite bird indices are transparent, fairly simple, and easy for policymakers and the public to understand [10].
- Because most birds fly, they can track pulsed resources, and migrants connect ecosystem processes across long distances [8].

**Weaknesses**

- Birds range widely, so the area a bird "samples" is large and poorly defined [6].
- They are long-lived, so pollutant burdens build up in complicated ways, and their physiology may regulate pollutant levels better than that of invertebrates [6].
- Sampling birds is harder, and killing them can be unacceptable on conservation or ethical grounds [6].
- Past monitoring programmes often had unclear aims and weak methods [2].

## Pollution in food webs

- Furness argues that the traits usually counted against birds as pollutant monitors (mobility, long life) are also what let them integrate pollution across areas, time and food webs, provided the species is chosen carefully [6].
- Radionuclides are less studied in birds than heavy metals or organic compounds, partly because contamination has been confined to a few sites such as weapons production and test sites [1, ch. 4].
- Becker's chapter on biomonitoring with birds could not be read. Its keywords (heavy metal contamination, biomagnification, raptors, seabirds, long-term monitoring) show the topic but give no findings [5].

## Marine prey stocks

- Seabirds reflect marine productivity and biological interactions, and the chapter reviews bird-based indicators of fish stock condition along with their limits [1, ch. 6].
- The author recommends more research on what seabirds eat and how they are linked to their prey [1, ch. 6].

## Freshwater quality

- Birds are abundant, conspicuous and diverse in freshwater systems and their margins [1, ch. 5].
- Inland waters cover only a few percent of the land surface, yet 11-23% of all bird species use them or their margins at some time of year [1, ch. 5].

## Habitat condition and disturbance

- Canterbury and colleagues sorted forest birds into species typical of disturbed habitat (shrubland, forest edge) and species typical of mature forest, then combined their relative abundances into a "bird community index" [4].
- They surveyed 197 plots in loblolly-shortleaf pine forest from Georgia to Virginia, and analysed 90 randomly chosen plots in detail [4].
- Species within each group responded consistently to disturbance, which supports using the groups for monitoring [4].
- Most of the sampled area was dominated by disturbance-tolerant birds; sites dominated by mature-forest birds were uncommon [4].

## Wider biodiversity (composite "wild bird indicators")

How the index is built:

- Each species' population index is log-transformed, averaged across species, and transformed back, which gives a geometric mean [10]. With $n$ species and $I_{i,t}$ the index of species $i$ in year $t$ (base year set to 100):

$$I_t = \exp\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln I_{i,t}\right)$$

- The UK indicator covered 1970-1999, drew on eight data sources, and used 139 common native species for the headline index, split by habitat (farmland, woodland, wetland, coastal, urban, upland) [10].
- The European indicator combines national breeding bird survey trends in the same way [11].

What the indices show:

- UK, 1970-1999: the all-species index stayed roughly level, but farmland birds fell by about 40% and woodland birds by about 20% from the mid-1970s [10].
- Farmland and woodland together cover about 85% of UK land, so these two groups describe most of the country [10].
- Rare native species rose by more than 260% between 1973 and 1998, and wetland birds increased overall [10].
- In a review of 254 multi-species bird indicators from 47 studies (2011-2016), about half showed declines and about 20% showed increases [9].

Policy use:

- The UK Government adopted the index as one of 15 headline "Quality of Life" indicators and committed to reversing farmland bird declines by 2020 [10].
- Governments and the EU use wild bird indicators to judge sustainable development and ecosystem health [11].

## Ecosystem functioning

What birds do:

- Birds act as predators (including of crop pests), pollinators, scavengers, seed dispersers, seed predators and ecosystem engineers [8].
- They supply all four categories of ecosystem service in the Millennium Ecosystem Assessment: provisioning, regulating, cultural and supporting [8].

What bird losses signal (figures from a third-party copy of [7], not the publisher's version):

- 21% of bird species are extinction-prone and 6.5% are functionally extinct, meaning they contribute almost nothing to ecosystem processes [7].
- By 2100, the intermediate scenario projects 6-14% of species extinct and 7-25% functionally extinct. On oceanic islands, 28-56% could be functionally extinct [7].
- Frugivores, herbivores, nectarivores, piscivores and scavengers face higher-than-average projected extinction, and specialised species are at greater risk [7].
- Decomposition, pollination and seed dispersal are expected to decline as a result [7].
- The Stanford press release on the study gives an example: after India's vulture collapse in the 1990s, feral dogs and rats increased, with a rise in rabies deaths [7, press release].

# Can these indications be linked to environmental change?

## Where the link is made

| Environmental change | Evidence in the sources | Strength as presented |
|------------------|-----------------------------|------------------|
| Agricultural intensification and land-use change | UK farmland birds down about 40%; authors attribute this mainly to intensification, and note parallel land-use-driven declines in farmland invertebrates and plants [10]. About 70% of reviewed bird indicators address land-use change only [9] | Strongest in this set, but rests on studies cited by [10], not on the index itself |
| Human disturbance of forest | Bird community index tracks a disturbance gradient across 197 plots [4] | Clear association in space; a snapshot, not a change measured over time |
| Chemical and radionuclide pollution | Birds integrate pollutant exposure over area, time and food web [6]; contamination studied at restricted sites [1, ch. 4] | Argued in principle in the abstracts; case details not read |
| Changes in marine prey stocks | Seabirds reflect marine productivity [1, ch. 6] | Reviewed with stated limits; details not read |
| Climate change | A minority of bird indicators address climate, including the Climate Impact Indicator [9]; climate is one named driver of bird decline [7] | Less developed than land use in the reviewed indicators |
| Combined human pressures | Habitat loss, fragmentation, exploitation, introduced species, disease and climate change drive bird declines; half of threatened species face threats besides habitat loss [7] | Global model based on IUCN threat categories |

## Why the link needs care

- **Correlation, not experiment.** Monitoring has relied mainly on correlational analysis instead of the manipulative experiments ecologists prefer [2].
- **Causes need population knowledge.** Finding the likely cause of a decline requires understanding the underlying population processes [1, ch. 7].
- **Local versus distant causes.** Pairing a bird index with a habitat index can help separate local habitat effects from landscape or wintering-ground effects [4].
- **Averages can hide change.** In a composite index, gains in common species can offset declines in threatened ones [10].
- **A rising index is not always good news.** Bird gains can reflect habitat degradation, for example mild eutrophication [10].
- **No built-in target.** A composite index gives a trend but no reference state to compare against [10].
- **Birds are one component.** Wild bird indicators measure only part of biodiversity change and should be used carefully [11]. The link between birds and wider biodiversity is described as open to question [10].
- **Statistical gaps.** About 30% of reviewed indicators ignore annual random variation and only about 15% account for detectability [9]. The UK index had no precision estimate, and long gap-filling by extrapolation is questionable [10].
- **Narrow coverage.** About 83% of reviewed indicators are Europe-only, more than 80% use breeding-season data only, and farmland and forest dominate [9].
- **Projections are conservative models.** The global extinction figures treat IUCN-based probabilities as lower bounds and exclude near-threatened species [7].
- **Birds may be a best case.** Other vertebrate groups are 1.7-2.5 times more threatened than birds [7].

# Gaps in this review

- **Koskimies 1989 [3]** could not be read. A related abstract by the same author (Koskimies 1988, *Statistical Journal of the UN Economic Commission for Europe*, DOI 10.3233/SJU-1988-5303) describes monitoring as continuous quantitative research with standardised methods that reveals changes, and causes of changes, in bird abundance and ecology, based on Finnish work. This is a different paper and is given only as a pointer.
- **Becker 2003 [5]** could not be read, so the report has no findings from it.
- **Chapters 1 and 3 of the 1993 book [2, 6]** were read as abstracts only. Their case studies, species, tissues sampled and numbers are missing here.
- **Sekercioglu et al. 2004 [7]** was read through a third-party copy and a press release. The two disagree on how threatened insectivores are, so that point is left out. Figures should be checked against the PNAS version before being quoted.
- **Whelan et al. 2008 [8]** and **Gregory & van Strien 2010 [11]** were read as abstracts only, so their examples and trend figures are missing.

These papers are best opened through a university library login to fill the gaps.
