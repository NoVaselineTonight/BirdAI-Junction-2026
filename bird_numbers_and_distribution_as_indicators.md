# Summary

This version looks at the sources through two measures only:

1. **How many birds there are** (abundance): counts of birds repeated every year and turned into a trend.
2. **Which species live where** (distribution and community make-up): which species occur in a habitat, a site or a region, and how that mix shifts.

What the sources show:

- **Numbers are the main working indicator.** Bird counts are combined into multi-species indices, and half of 254 such indicators reviewed for 2011-2016 show declines [9].
- **Numbers only become meaningful once species are sorted by where they live.** The UK all-species index stayed level for 1970-1999, while farmland birds fell by about 40% and woodland birds by about 20% [10].
- **The species mix at a site is an indicator on its own.** The balance of mature-forest species against disturbance-tolerant species tracks how disturbed a forest is [4].
- **Both measures can be linked to environmental change**, mainly land use. Land-use change is the driver studied in about 70% of reviewed indicators, and climate change alone in 11% [9]. The farmland decline is attributed mainly to agricultural intensification [10].
- **Main caveat:** the link is statistical, not experimental [2], and a rising count is not always a sign of a healthier ecosystem [10].

**Read this before relying on the report.** Most publisher sites blocked automated reading. Only two sources were read as full text [9, 10], and most of this version rests on them. Seven were read as abstracts or summaries and two not at all [3, 5]. The access table is at the end. Every statement carries the number of its source.

# Part 1: Bird numbers as the indicator

## How birds are counted

- National breeding bird surveys count birds each year using standard field methods, and those counts are the raw material for the European wild bird indicator [11].
- The UK indicator drew on eight data sources for 1970-1999, using either absolute counts (such as pairs) or relative indices [10].
- Different bird groups need different counts [10]:
    - common land birds: mapping surveys of breeding birds (Common Birds Census, 69 species);
    - waterside birds: the Waterways Bird Survey (four specialist species, including Common Kingfisher and Dipper);
    - rare breeders: pair counts reported to the Rare Breeding Birds Panel;
    - seabirds: a full colony census about every 15 years, plus sample plots since 1986;
    - waterfowl: winter counts.
- Most bird indicators in the wider literature use breeding-season census data [9].

## How counts become one index

- Each species' counts are converted to an index with the first year set to 100 [10].
- The species indices are then log-transformed, averaged, and transformed back, which gives a geometric mean [10]. With $n$ species and $I_{i,t}$ the index of species $i$ in year $t$:

$$I_t = \exp\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln I_{i,t}\right)$$

- Every species counts equally, so a rare or vulnerable species has the same weight as a common or pest species [10].
- The European indicator is built the same way from national survey trends [11].

## Why count birds instead of listing species

- A list of species can stay the same while numbers change underneath it, so a species count alone can miss real change [10].
- Where trend data on numbers existed, the UK authors always used them in preference to other data, even over shorter periods [10].

## What the numbers have shown

| Group counted | Change in numbers | Period and place | Source |
|----------------------|---------------------|----------------|----------|
| All common native species (139) | Roughly level | UK, 1970-1999 | [10] |
| Farmland birds (20 species) | Down about 40% | UK, from mid-1970s | [10] |
| Woodland birds (41 species) | Down about 20% | UK, from mid-1970s | [10] |
| Wetland birds (15 species) | Up overall | UK, 1970-1999 | [10] |
| Rare native species (under 500 pairs; 42 species) | Up more than 260% | UK, 1973-1998 | [10] |
| Introduced or feral species (11) | Up strongly | UK, 1970-1999 | [10] |
| 254 multi-species indicators | 50% declining, about 20% rising, about 20% no clear trend | Mostly Europe, studies from 2011-2016 | [9] |
| All birds worldwide (individuals) | Down an estimated 20-25%, while only 1.3% of species went extinct | Global, since 1500 | [7, press release] |

## What those numbers say about the ecosystem

- **Farmland.** The fall in farmland bird numbers is described as probably a sign of wider environmental deterioration, and farmland invertebrates and plants declined in parallel [10].
- **The whole country.** Farmland and woodland cover about 85% of UK land and hold many of the most abundant species, so these two indices describe most of the landscape [10].
- **Seas.** Seabirds are top consumers, so their numbers can reflect marine productivity and the state of fish stocks [1, ch. 6].
- **Rare species are a different signal.** Much of the rise in rare species is put down to direct conservation action, so it was kept out of the headline index as unrepresentative [10].
- **Numbers fall before species disappear.** The global estimate above shows far larger losses of individual birds than of species [7, press release].

## Limits of using numbers

- **A level total can hide losses.** Gains in common species can offset declines in threatened ones [10].
- **More birds is not always better.** Gains can reflect degradation, for example mild eutrophication [10].
- **No target is built in.** The index shows a trend but not what a healthy level would be [10].
- **Not every bird is detected.** Only 15% of reviewed indicators (38 of 254) modelled detectability, and shifts in detectability over time can bias abundance estimates [9].
- **Uncertainty is often missing.** About 28% of indicators with a stated statistical approach assessed no uncertainty [9]. The UK index had no precision estimate [10].
- **The starting year matters.** The authors of the review recommend reducing sensitivity to the baseline year [9].

# Part 2: Which species live where

## Sorting species by habitat

- In the UK indicator, each species was assigned to one habitat by its main breeding-season foraging habitat: coastal, farmland, woodland, wetland, urban, upland, or unclassified [10].
- Among species with more than 500 pairs, this gave 20 farmland, 41 woodland, 15 wetland and 63 other or unclassified species [10].
- Across the reviewed literature, species selection is used in 85% of indicators (215 of 254) and site selection in about half [9].
- Species are often grouped by habitat preference, and some indicators compare species that gain from a driver with species that lose from it [9].

## The species mix as an indicator of habitat condition

- Canterbury and colleagues split forest birds into species typical of mature forest and species typical of disturbed habitat (shrubland, forest edge) [4].
- The relative abundance of the two groups forms a "bird community index" of forest condition [4].
- Across 197 plots in pine forest from Georgia to Virginia, species within each group responded consistently to disturbance [4].
- Most of the sampled area was dominated by disturbance-tolerant species; sites dominated by mature-forest species were uncommon [4].

## Range maps (atlases) as a stand-in for counts

- For 42 of 219 UK species, about 19%, there were no count data. Change in breeding range between the 1968-72 and 1988-91 atlases was used instead [10].
- The authors judge range a weak substitute [10]:
    - range change and change in numbers may not move together, and the relation may differ by species;
    - range is a relatively insensitive measure of population trend;
    - they expected atlas data to be dropped from later updates.

## Differences between regions

- Indicators for England and Scotland differ considerably from each other, partly because the two hold different species [10].
- About 83% of reviewed indicators cover Europe only, so little is known from this method about other regions [9].

## Where species live and climate

- The Community Temperature Index and the Climate Impact Indicator are climate-pressure indicators built from bird data, and the EU has adopted the second [9].
- The review does not explain how either is calculated, so this report cannot describe the method [9].

## Habitats that hold many or few species

- Inland waters cover only a few percent of the land surface, yet 11-23% of all bird species use them or their margins at some time of year [1, ch. 5].
- Farmland and forest account for about 40% of reviewed indicators. Wetland, grassland, shrubland, marine and urban indicators are rare [9].

## Places and species types at greatest risk

Figures from a third-party copy of [7], not the publisher's version:

- **Islands.** On oceanic islands, 28-56% of species could be functionally extinct by 2100, mainly because island species have small ranges [7].
- **Specialists.** More specialised species have a higher extinction risk [7].
- **Feeding groups.** Fruit-eaters, plant-eaters, nectar-feeders, fish-eaters and scavengers face higher-than-average projected losses [7].
- **What disappears with them.** Where these groups are lost, seed dispersal, pollination and decomposition are expected to decline [7]. These are among the services birds provide as predators, pollinators, scavengers and seed dispersers [8].

## Birds that live in more than one place

- Migratory species connect ecosystem processes across great distances [8].
- For migrants, a driver can act at different stages of the life cycle, and indicators outside the breeding season are scarce [9].
- A local count can therefore reflect conditions elsewhere. Pairing a bird index with a habitat index helps separate local habitat effects from landscape or wintering-ground effects [4].

# Part 4: Can these signals be linked to environmental change?

## Which changes the indicators are tied to

| Driver | Share of 254 reviewed indicators | Share of those declining | Source |
|------------------------|------------------|------------------|------|
| Land-use change only | About 70% (177) | About 40% | [9] |
| Climate change only | 11% (27) | About 70% (climate and combined together) | [9] |
| Climate and land use combined | 19% (47) | About 70% (climate and combined together) | [9] |

- **Agricultural intensification.** The UK farmland decline is attributed mainly to intensification, on the strength of other studies the authors cite [10].
- **Human disturbance of forest.** The forest bird community index follows a disturbance gradient [4]. This is a comparison between places at one time, not a change measured over years.
- **Climate change.** Fewer indicators address climate, but a larger share of them are declining [9].
- **Changes in marine prey.** Seabirds reflect marine productivity, with stated limits [1, ch. 6].
- **Combined human pressures.** Habitat loss, exploitation, introduced species, disease and climate change are named as drivers of global bird decline [7].
- **Policy use.** The UK Government adopted the index as one of 15 headline "Quality of Life" indicators and pledged to reverse farmland bird declines by 2020 [10]. Governments and the EU use wild bird indicators to judge ecosystem health [11].

## Why the link needs care

- **Correlation, not experiment.** Monitoring relies mainly on correlational analysis [2].
- **Causes need population knowledge.** Naming the likely cause of a decline requires understanding the underlying population processes [1, ch. 7].
- **Birds are one component.** Wild bird indicators measure only part of biodiversity change [11], and the link between birds and wider biodiversity is described as open to question [10].
- **Land use and climate are rarely modelled together.** The review recommends putting both in the same framework [9].
- **Coverage is narrow.** More than 80% of indicators use breeding-season data only [9].
